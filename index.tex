\title{Science Has an Objective Function}
\author{Greg Coppola}
\date{2026}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
Science has always had an objective function. It has never been written down
in a single place. Written down, it is: minimize $L(H) + L(D \mid H)$, where
$H$ is a hypothesis, $D$ is observed data, $L(H)$ is the description length
of the hypothesis (approximating its Kolmogorov complexity), and
$L(D \mid H)$ is the description length of the data given the hypothesis
(the negative log-likelihood). This is the Minimum Description Length (MDL)
principle---the computable approximation to Solomonoff induction, which is
Bayesian inference with the Kolmogorov/Solomonoff prior. We argue that this
framework is not a new proposal but a formal statement of what science has
always been doing informally. We develop the framework from first principles,
trace the history of philosophy of science as a sequence of partial
approximations to it, and identify ten cases where ideas from different
traditions---philosophy, computability theory, information theory,
neuroscience, machine learning---turn out to be the same idea, expressed in
different language. We close with the observation that two current
developments---autonomous AI research systems and machine-verified formal
proof---are direct engineering instantiations of the framework, and that
the tools now exist to approximate the ideal at scale.
\end{abstract}
